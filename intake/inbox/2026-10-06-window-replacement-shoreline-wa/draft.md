## Key Takeaways

- The City of Shoreline's adopted building code exempts window replacement from a building permit only when no structural elements are revised and emergency escape and rescue openings are not reduced in size — enlarge an opening, move a header, or shrink a bedroom egress window and the project moves into permit territory ([Shoreline Municipal Code Chapter 15.05](https://ecode360.com/49772285)).
- If your Shoreline home was built before 1978, removing old sashes and trim can disturb lead-based paint. Washington requires paid renovators working on pre-1978 homes to hold Renovation, Repair and Painting (RRP) certification through the [Washington State Department of Commerce](https://www.commerce.wa.gov/lead-based-paint/rrp/).
- Check every window contractor and installation crew on [Washington L&I Verify](https://secure.lni.wa.gov/verify/) yourself before a deposit — registration, bond, and insurance are public records.

## Shoreline house stock and why windows wear out here

Shoreline sits between north Seattle and south Snohomish County, with neighborhoods that run from Richmond Beach above Puget Sound to the corridors along Aurora Avenue and the newer station areas near the light rail line. Much of the housing is mid-century ranches, split-levels, and daylight-basement homes, mixed with newer townhomes and infill. In older homes it is common to find original single-pane wood or aluminum sashes, or first-generation insulated units whose seals have failed.

The symptoms are familiar: fog between panes, condensation and mildew on sills each winter, soft wood at the bottom rail, cold drafts near bedroom windows, and exterior caulk that has cracked away from the trim. On homes facing the Sound, wind-driven rain tests every joint. Under the heavy tree canopy found in many Shoreline neighborhoods, shaded elevations stay damp long after a storm passes.

That is why window replacement in Shoreline should be treated as a building-envelope project, not a finish swap. The glass is the part you see; the rough opening, flashing, and the transition to the weather-resistive barrier behind the siding decide whether the wall stays dry. ## When the City of Shoreline requires a permit

Shoreline adopts the state building codes with local amendments in [Shoreline Municipal Code Chapter 15.05](https://ecode360.com/49772285). Among the work it lists as exempt from a permit is "replacement of windows where there are no structural elements being revised and emergency escape and rescue openings are not reduced in size." The same exemption appears in both the residential code and the building code amendments.

Read that language as a test, not a blanket pass:

- **Same size, same location, no structural change** — typically exempt from a building permit.
- **Enlarging, moving, or adding an opening** — changes framing and usually a header, so plan on a building permit and plan review.
- **Bedroom egress** — a replacement unit that shrinks the clear opening of an emergency escape window below what the code requires fails the exemption and creates a safety problem. Ask for measured clear-opening dimensions, not rough catalog sizes.
- **Energy code** — even exempt replacements should meet current Washington energy code performance for the glass and frame; ask for the U-factor on the written quote.

An exemption from a building permit does not exempt the work from the code itself, and it does not cover other work bundled into the same job, such as new wiring or siding replacement. Confirm the parcel is inside Shoreline city limits before relying on city rules; the Board's [permits](../permits.html) guide covers how to check jurisdiction.

## Lead-safe work on pre-1978 homes

Window sashes, stops, and exterior trim are some of the most commonly painted and repainted surfaces in an older house, and window work creates friction and dust. In Washington, the [Department of Commerce Renovation, Repair and Painting program](https://www.commerce.wa.gov/lead-based-paint/rrp/) requires firms that perform renovation, repair, or painting work for compensation on pre-1978 homes to hold firm certification, with a certified renovator performing or supervising the work.

For a Shoreline homeowner, that means three practical questions before signing:

1. **Does the firm hold Washington RRP firm certification?** Commerce publishes certified-company lists on its [homeowner and business resources page](https://www.commerce.wa.gov/lead-based-paint/lbp-resources/).
2. **Who is the certified renovator on site?** Ask for the name, and ask how containment, cleanup, and verification will be handled at each opening.
3. **How will debris leave the property?** Old sashes and trim should be wrapped and removed, not broken up in the yard.

If the house was built in 1978 or later, this section may not apply, but it is still worth asking how dust will be controlled inside occupied rooms.

## Insert or full-frame: choosing the scope

Two installation methods dominate residential replacement:

**Insert (pocket) replacement** sets a new unit inside the existing frame. It keeps interior and exterior trim intact and is faster, but it reduces glass area slightly and depends on the old frame being sound and dry.

**Full-frame replacement** removes the old frame down to the rough opening. It costs more and disturbs trim, but it lets the crew find hidden rot, rebuild the sill, and integrate new flashing with the weather-resistive barrier. When Shoreline homes show soft sills, staining below the window, or chronic leaks, full-frame is usually the stronger choice.

A useful sequence:

1. **Audit every opening.** Separate air leakage, water intrusion, and failed seals; they point to different fixes.
2. **Decide method per window.** Mixed scopes are common — insert on sound frames, full-frame where the wall is wet.
3. **Get performance in writing.** The quote should list U-factor and Solar Heat Gain Coefficient for each unit, the flashing products, and the sealant.
4. **Plan for weather.** Fall and winter installs need a plan for openings left open overnight, and who owns tarping and temporary boarding.

## Flashing that drains, not just seals

Most long-term window failures start at the bottom corners of the rough opening. A good installation uses a sloped or pan-flashed sill, jamb and head flashing lapped shingle-style so water always runs outward, and a deliberate gap at the bottom of the unit so any water that gets behind the frame can escape. Sealing the bottom edge shut can trap water against framing — a common mistake in quick replacements.

## How to shortlist and verify installers

Ask each bidder for recent Shoreline or north King County window projects, photos of sill and head flashing (not only finished interiors), and a written scope that names insert or full-frame per opening.

Walk through the Board's [verify a contractor](../verify-contractor.html) checklist. Use [Washington L&I Verify](https://secure.lni.wa.gov/verify/) to confirm registration, bond, and insurance for the general contractor and any specialty window crew. For pre-1978 homes, confirm Washington RRP firm certification as well. Prefer written scopes that list flashing products, interior protection, trim restoration, disposal of old units, and who pays if a permit becomes necessary after an opening is changed.

## Next steps for a Shoreline window project

1. **Audit** — walk every elevation and note fogged glass, drafts, soft sills, and stained siding below windows.
2. **Test the exemption** — same size and location with no structural change and no smaller egress openings, or a building permit under [SMC 15.05](https://ecode360.com/49772285).
3. **Check the house age** — pre-1978 homes need a Washington RRP-certified firm.
4. **Verify and compare** — L&I Verify results, flashing details, and performance values in writing before any deposit.

Treat Shoreline's code language, older house stock, and wet weather as design inputs from the start, and the new windows will keep the wall dry long after the installer leaves.
