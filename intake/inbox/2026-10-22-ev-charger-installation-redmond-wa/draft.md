## Key Takeaways

- **Redmond issues its own electrical permits.** Washington L&I's [list of cities that run their own electrical permits and inspections](https://lni.wa.gov/licensing-permits/electrical/electrical-permits-fees-and-inspections/city-electrical-permits-inspections) includes Redmond, and the City's [Electrical/Mechanical/Plumbing Permit page](https://www.redmond.gov/484/ElectricalMechanicalPlumbing-Permit) lists a "new or altered electrical circuit, panel, or service" as work that needs a permit. A new 240-volt circuit for a Level 2 charger is exactly that.
- **Capacity comes before hardware.** The U.S. Department of Energy's [home charging guide](https://afdc.energy.gov/fuels/electricity-charging-home) notes that some homes do not have enough electrical capacity for Level 2 equipment and that the National Electrical Code treats EV charging as a continuous load. [PSE](https://www.pse.com/en/rebates/ev-home-charger) asks that you or your installer confirm your existing service can support a new charger before it goes in.
- **Rebates have rules.** PSE's [home charging program](https://www.pse.com/en/pages/electric-cars/Home-charging) ties its charger rebate to a pre-qualified, Wi-Fi-connected ENERGY STAR Smart Level 2 model, a PSE residential electric account, and an application deadline after installation. Check every installer on [Washington L&I Verify](https://secure.lni.wa.gov/verify/) before you sign.

## Why EV charging questions keep coming up in Redmond

Redmond has a lot of drivers who commute across the Eastside, a wide range of housing from older split-levels to newer townhouses, and plenty of households with a plug-in hybrid or full electric car in the garage. Most of those cars arrive with a Level 1 cordset that plugs into an ordinary 120-volt outlet. For some owners that is enough. The Department of Energy's [Alternative Fuels Data Center](https://afdc.energy.gov/fuels/electricity-charging-home) says many drivers meet their daily range by charging overnight on Level 1, provided a dedicated branch circuit outlet is near the parking spot.

For longer commutes, larger batteries or two electric cars in one household, a Level 2 (240-volt) charger is the usual next step. That is where the project stops being "buy a charger" and becomes a small electrical job with a permit, an inspection, a load question at the panel and, sometimes, a conversation with the utility.

The Board of Project Stewardship's [Redmond directory](../redmond.html), [electrician page](../electrician.html), [permits overview](../permits.html) and [verify a contractor checklist](../verify-contractor.html) cover the wider picture. This guide sticks to what the City of Redmond, Washington L&I, the Washington Administrative Code, the U.S. Department of Energy, ENERGY STAR and Puget Sound Energy actually publish.

## Do you need a permit in Redmond?

In most Washington cities, L&I sells electrical permits and sends the inspector. Redmond is different. L&I's [city electrical permits list](https://lni.wa.gov/licensing-permits/electrical/electrical-permits-fees-and-inspections/city-electrical-permits-inspections) tells anyone working inside the city limits of a listed city to contact that city to buy the electrical permit and request the inspection, and Redmond is on the list with its permit counter at 15670 NE 85th St (425-556-2473).

The City's [Electrical/Mechanical/Plumbing Permit page](https://www.redmond.gov/484/ElectricalMechanicalPlumbing-Permit) lists the kinds of work that need a permit, including:

- a new or altered electrical circuit, panel or service;
- a new or replacement heat pump, furnace or water heater (mechanical and plumbing permits).

Redmond's [Electrical Permit Application](https://www.redmond.gov/DocumentCenter/View/2198/Electrical-Application-PDF) says it in one line: an electrical permit is required before electrical equipment is installed, altered, replaced or remodeled. The application asks for the value of the electrical work, the number of new circuits, and whether a service, feeder or subpanel is being added or altered.

What that means for a charger:

- **Plugging the car's Level 1 cordset into an existing outlet** is not a new installation. Nothing is being wired.
- **A new 240-volt receptacle or a hardwired Level 2 charger** means a new circuit, so plan on a Redmond electrical permit and inspection.
- **A charger that also needs a subpanel, a feeder to a detached garage, or a service upgrade** adds those items to the same permit, and a service change may also involve the utility.

### How to apply

The City's permit page says residential electrical, mechanical and plumbing permits are over-the-counter permits that can be applied for online through the [Redmond ePermitting Service (REPS)](https://www.redmond.gov/1114/Redmond-ePermitting-Service-REPS), which requires registration. If a permit type is not available online, the City asks for the application form by email. In-person intake is first-come, first-served, with staff available Monday through Thursday from 10 a.m. to 3 p.m., and appointments can be requested by email. The City does not accept paper applications or plans.

Redmond's application also has a short list of work that triggers plan review. It says plan review is not required for one- and two-family dwellings and IRC townhouses in general, but it does list "all exterior appliance installations including Photovoltaic Systems and generators" among the items that require it. If your charger is going on an outside wall, a post or a pedestal rather than inside the garage, ask the permit technicians how they will treat it before you schedule the work.

Fees in Redmond are based on the City's published schedule; the permit page points to the [Development Services fee schedule](https://www.redmond.gov/504/Development-Services-Fees) and invites homeowners to email the permit technicians for a fee estimate. The permit card and plans, where plans apply, have to be kept on site.

### Which code applies

Washington adopts the National Electrical Code by rule. [WAC 296-46B-010](https://app.leg.wa.gov/wac/default.aspx?cite=296-46B-010) adopts the 2023 edition of the NEC and says the 2026 edition takes over effective December 31, 2026, with the edition in force tied to the permit issue date. Cities that run their own electrical programs, like Redmond, enforce the same baseline. The Department of Energy points out that most charging requirements sit in NEC Article 625 and that a charger is a continuous load, which is why electricians size the circuit above the charger's rated output.

## Panel and service capacity: the question that decides the job

The charger itself is usually the easy part. The hard question is whether the house can feed it.

The [Alternative Fuels Data Center](https://afdc.energy.gov/fuels/electricity-charging-home) says electricians can tell homeowners whether their home has enough capacity for vehicle charging, that some homes do not, and that a qualified electrician can add circuits to accommodate Level 2 charging. In practice, a good installer looks at:

- **The service size and the main breaker.** The service rating sets the ceiling for everything in the house.
- **Existing large loads.** Electric range, dryer, water heater, heat pump or furnace, hot tub, shop tools — anything that runs at the same time as an evening charging session.
- **Open spaces in the panel.** A full panel may need a subpanel or a different breaker layout, and some older panels are not worth adding to.
- **The route from panel to parking spot.** Distance, wall and ceiling access, and whether the run crosses a finished space or goes outdoors.
- **Future loads.** A second EV, a heat pump conversion or a kitchen remodel with an induction range can change the answer.

PSE's [home charger rebate page](https://www.pse.com/en/rebates/ev-home-charger) asks that you or your installer contact its Customer Construction Services team (1-888-321-7779) when adding a brand-new charger, to confirm your existing service can support it. PSE notes there may be additional fees if your service has to be upgraded and that it is critical to check local building codes for installation requirements. If the answer is "the service is too small," you are no longer pricing a charger; you are pricing a service upgrade, and that changes both the permit and the schedule.

Many smart chargers can be set to a lower current so they fit on a smaller circuit, and some can manage their output against other household loads. Ask the installer what current setting the circuit is designed for, and make sure the charger's setting matches the breaker.

## Picking a location: garage, driveway or outside wall

The [Department of Energy](https://afdc.energy.gov/fuels/electricity-charging-home) says home charging equipment is often installed in garages, but outdoor installation and use are also safe, even in the rain, as long as the equipment is outdoor-rated. In a Redmond winter that matters. Think about:

- **Where the car's charge port sits** when it is parked the way you actually park.
- **Cable length and trip hazards** across walkways, stairs and doorways.
- **Protection from the car.** A charger mounted where a bumper can reach it needs to be moved or guarded.
- **Weather exposure.** An outdoor unit and its wiring need to be rated for the location, and the City may treat an exterior installation differently at plan review, as noted above.
- **Wi-Fi reach.** PSE's rebate requires a Wi-Fi-connected charger, so a garage with no signal may need an extender.

## Plug-in or hardwired?

Level 2 chargers come in two broad styles:

- **Plug-in models** use a 240-volt receptacle. They are easy to replace or take with you, and the receptacle has to be the right type and rated for the charger.
- **Hardwired models** connect directly to the circuit with no receptacle. They are common for higher-current chargers and outdoor locations.

Both need a dedicated circuit sized for a continuous load, and both need a Redmond electrical permit when the circuit is new. Ask the installer which style they recommend for your panel and parking spot, and why. PSE's list of [qualified chargers](https://www.pse.com/en/rebates/ev-home-charger) includes both hardwired and plug-in versions of several models, so the rebate does not have to decide the style for you.

## PSE home charging rebates: what the rules say

If your home gets its electricity from Puget Sound Energy (check your bill), PSE's [Up & Go Electric Home Charging program](https://www.pse.com/en/pages/electric-cars/Home-charging) offers a rebate on a qualified Level 2 charger, and increased rebates plus installation incentives for customers with qualifying household incomes. Its [rebate rules](https://www.pse.com/en/rebates/ev-home-charger) say, among other things, that you must:

- be a current PSE residential electric customer, with the charger installed in PSE's electric service area;
- own, lease or have ordered an EV or plug-in hybrid;
- purchase, install and Wi-Fi connect an ENERGY STAR Smart Level 2 charger that PSE has pre-qualified;
- submit the application within 60 days of installation, with a receipt showing the retailer, date, manufacturer, model number, price and proof of payment;
- accept one rebate per qualified single-family residence within a five-year window.

For income-qualified customers whose charger is professionally installed, PSE asks for an installation receipt showing the date, description of work, price and proof of payment. Rebate amounts and terms change, so read the current page before you buy, and keep every receipt.

[ENERGY STAR's EV charger page](https://www.energystar.gov/products/ev_chargers) has a product finder for certified models, and the Department of Energy recommends buying safety-certified equipment tested by a nationally recognized testing laboratory.

## What to write into a Redmond EV charger bid

A clear bid makes the job easy to compare and easy to inspect. Ask each installer to spell out:

- **Load assessment.** Service size, panel condition, existing large loads, and whether the house can take the charger as designed. If they recommend a service upgrade or subpanel, they should say why in writing.
- **Circuit design.** Breaker size, conductor type and size, wiring method (cable or conduit), receptacle type if any, and the current setting the charger will be configured to.
- **Route and finish work.** Where the wiring runs, what walls or ceilings get opened, and who patches and paints.
- **Location details.** Mounting height and position, protection from vehicle impact, outdoor rating where needed, and Wi-Fi.
- **Permit and inspection.** Who applies through Redmond's REPS portal, what the permit covers, and that the work is not done until the City inspection passes.
- **Utility coordination.** Whether PSE's Customer Construction Services was contacted and what they said about the existing service.
- **Charger supply.** Whether you buy the charger (and claim any rebate) or the installer supplies it, and which model, so you can check it against PSE's qualified list before purchase.

## How to shortlist and verify an installer

Get two or three written bids built on the list above. Then:

- Look up each company on [Washington L&I Verify](https://secure.lni.wa.gov/verify/) and confirm the person doing the wiring holds the right electrical license and the company holds an electrical contractor license, bond and insurance.
- Redmond's [electrical application](https://www.redmond.gov/DocumentCenter/View/2198/Electrical-Application-PDF) asks for the contractor's Washington license number and whether they hold a Redmond business license. Ask for both before work starts.
- Ask to see the permit in REPS once it is issued, and do not close up walls or ceilings before the inspection.
- Walk through the Board's [verify a contractor](../verify-contractor.html) and [red flags](../red-flags-hiring.html) pages before you sign.

## Next steps for a Redmond EV charger

1. Decide whether Level 1 on a dedicated outlet is enough for how you actually drive; if not, plan for Level 2.
2. Note where you park, where the charge port is, and how far that is from the electrical panel.
3. Have an electrician assess service size and panel capacity, and ask PSE's Customer Construction Services to confirm the service can support a new charger.
4. Pick a charger model, and if you want a PSE rebate, confirm it is on PSE's qualified list before you buy.
5. Get written bids that cover the circuit, route, location, permit and inspection.
6. Verify every firm on [L&I Verify](https://secure.lni.wa.gov/verify/) and make sure the Redmond electrical permit is issued before the wiring starts.

A home charger looks like a small purchase. In Redmond it is really a short electrical project — capacity check, circuit design, City permit, inspection — and the installer who walks you through those steps in writing is usually the one who will do it right.
