The finished house, the ADU below it, and the garage. Tap a room to fly in, or drag to turn it.

Tap a label to learn what that part does.

Roof. The house gets one new low-slope roof of standing seam metal. It falls toward a gutter on the street side and overhangs the walls. The garage has its own metal roof. The plans call it a roof redesign because the old roof leaked.

Porch. The existing 60 SF open porch sits at the entry on the street side. The ADU gets a small new landing of its own at the basement level.

Deck. The existing deck runs along the main level on the downhill side, with a stair to grade. The plans replace its railings with glass railings and fold 27 SF of it into the dining room.

ADU. The new 865 SF accessory dwelling unit fills part of the existing basement, on the side where the lot falls away and the basement wall is out of the ground. It has a living room, bedroom, kitchen, bathroom, and laundry, and its own door.

Garage. A detached one-car garage, 12 by 20 feet inside, stands near the street with its own slab and a single overhead door. The plans give it a 12-foot height.

Foundation. The basement walls carry the house down to the footings. On the uphill side they hold back soil; on the downhill side they rise out of the ground with doors and windows.

Partitions. The inside walls divide the rooms. On a real house, check what sits on a wall before anyone takes it out. The remodel moves several of them.

Framing. Studs stand between a bottom plate and a top plate. That skeleton is the frame. Siding and the glass go on it later.

Headers. The tall windows and doors on the deck side take studs out of the wall. A header, a short beam across each opening, carries that load to the studs on both sides. This sketch does not size them.

Footings. The house and the garage send weight down their walls into the footings. Those footings have to suit that load. This sketch does not size them.

Structure. The main level sits on the basement walls: a floor, walls, and the roof above. A new roof has to tie into the frame already there.

Stairs. The existing stair runs from the main level down to the basement. A stair needs a clear path, headroom, and a rail.

Green-gray marks new work: the 27 SF dining addition, the new entry door with side lites, the new egress window, the glass deck railings, the electric fireplace and the ADU entry door. Furniture is drawn paler: it is placed only to show scale, and the permit sheets do not show it. Zoom in to see cabinet doors, faucets, trim and floor joints.

Homeowner practice

What a good steward does

Concrete habits that keep remodels, additions, and custom work accountable — whether you hire a design-build firm or self-manage trades.

Verify WA L&I

Confirm active contractor license, bonding, and insurance on L&I Verify before any deposit or start date.

Written scope

Insist on a written scope of work, allowances, and exclusions — not a handshake estimate — before demolition or ordering long-lead materials.

One steward contact

Name a single project steward for decisions and schedule. Avoid salesman-to-crew handoffs with no continuity.

Permit ownership

Know who pulls and owns each permit, who schedules inspections, and how corrections are closed before cover-up.

Change-order discipline

Require written change orders with price and schedule impact before extra work starts — no verbal “we’ll figure it out.”

Weather & dry-in

For additions and roof work, demand a dry-in plan: temporary weather protection, sequencing, and moisture checks before finishes.

Punch list before final pay

Walk a written punch list with photos, retain final payment until items are closed, and keep manuals/warranty contacts with the project file.

Pre-Construction Package

Show Details for Feasibility Study

Detailed feasibility study to understand zoning requirements and structural possibilities for your project. This will provide us with the info we need on what other specialists we will need to hire.

Specialists Included:

  • Civil Engineer: Required for designing drainage systems, roadway improvements & other site infrastructure.
  • Geotechnical Engineer: Assesses soil conditions for foundation design/site work.
  • Septic Design Engineer: Needed if property is not on municipal sewer.
  • Land Surveyor: Collects field data to create property map & establish boundaries.
  • Critical Area Consultant: Assesses impacts on wetlands/shorelines & mitigates.
  • Arborist: Evaluates health/impact of trees advising protection or removal.
Show Details for Permits

Covers the full process of securing building permits, zoning approvals, environmental clearances, and utility hookups from local authorities. Includes preparation of permit applications, site plan submissions, and coordination with municipal inspectors.

Timeline: 4–8 months.

Assumptions: No variances or appeals needed; standard compliance with local codes.

Show Details for Blueprints & Layouts

Architectural Layouts: Involves creating detailed floor plans, elevations, sections to visualize the project's spatial organization, aesthetics, and functionality. Ensures compliance with client vision and code requirements.

Show Details for Design & Materials

Material Selection & Specification Package:

  • 3D Renders of key project areas illustrating material application.
  • Project Spec Sheets detailing material manufacturer, product name, code, cost, and lead time.
  • Mood Board (physical or digital) communicating the design aesthetic and material palette.
Show Details for Geotechnical Engineering

Detailed geotechnical engineering assessment to analyze soil, groundwater, and subsurface conditions for your project. Ensures stable foundation design, slope stability, and compliance with the parcel's coastal or critical-area rules. Edmonds, Seattle, and county codes differ.

Rough Timeline (4-6 weeks):

  • Week 1: Initial site visit & proposal approval.
  • Weeks 2-3: Field investigations (borings) & sampling.
  • Weeks 4-5: Lab analysis & draft report.
  • Week 6: Final report delivery.

Specialists:

  • Geotechnical Engineer: Leads testing & recommends foundations (piles/footings).
  • Soil Technician: Conducts on-site borings.
  • Lab Analyst: Performs tests on soil bearing capacity/shear strength.
  • Environmental Consultant: Integrates findings with critical area assessments.
Show Details for Structural Engineering

Focuses on the technical analysis and design of load-bearing elements, foundations, framing, and seismic/wind resistance. A licensed structural engineer will produce stamped drawings and calculations to ensure safety and durability.

Show Details for Survey

Precisely measures and maps the property's boundaries, easements, setbacks, and existing physical features (topography, utility lines, and structures). Establishes horizontal and vertical controls needed for plans.
