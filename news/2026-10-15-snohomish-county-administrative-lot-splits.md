EVERETT, Wash. — Property owners in Snohomish County will be able to split a residential lot in two without going before the County Council, after the council unanimously approved a new administrative lot-split process Wednesday.

Ordinance 26-036 moves approval of residential lot splits to the county's Planning and Development Services department, The Daily Herald reported. The change removes steps that previously involved the council, including the need for a public hearing, though one may still be requested. The county's legislative record shows the council approved the ordinance as amended Wednesday; it is pending the county executive's signature.

"Sometimes the subdivision process takes a long time," project lead Michael Saponaro said at the meeting, according to the Herald. "So, the lot split is an administrative process that moves it more efficiently."

## What the new process allows

Under the ordinance, a single residential parcel can be divided into two buildable lots, provided development standards are met, according to the county's legislative summary. Saponaro told the council that both lots must be usable, only one new lot can be created per split, and either lot can be divided further later through the county's existing subdivision process. Required infrastructure improvements are limited to sidewalks, roads and other public right-of-way needs called for by code, he said.

The county's hearing notice, published Sept. 30, said the process would apply to residential properties in urban growth areas and summarized other parts of the proposal. Lot-split applications would be classified as Type 1 permits. Applications would have to be prepared by a registered professional land surveyor. Impact fees would not be required as a condition of approval for a preliminary lot split, and lot splits would be exempt from the county's five-year limit on subdividing the same land.

The notice said the council could also consider a substitute version. That version came out of input from the county's permitting divisions and handles development permits separately from the lot split itself. The original draft had allowed development to be included in the same application.

That separation matches how supporters described the change. Natalie Reber, a government affairs manager with the Master Builders Association of King and Snohomish Counties, spoke in favor at the hearing. She told the Herald that a landowner who wants to build on either new lot would still need further permits.

"Sometimes somebody can split the lot and make it a conservation space or something else," Reber said after the meeting. "We're hopeful it will create more homes."

## Following the state's lead

In 2025, the Legislature passed House Bill 1096, which required cities to make lot splitting an administrative process to encourage density and infill development. Counties were not required to follow suit, but Snohomish County used the bill as a starting point, Saponaro said.

The proposal went through review by county staff, the Master Builders Association, Snohomish County Public Works and the public, according to the council's legislative file. It received an executive recommendation Aug. 5, and the council continued the item twice that month before setting the October hearing.

Council Chair Megan Dunn said after the meeting that the change responds to affordability concerns.

"Now we can have smaller lots and more affordable-sized homes," Dunn said. She said a home that already has a detached accessory dwelling unit could have that unit split onto its own lot and sold separately.

Reber said the change could also help older residents who want to stay in their homes but downsize, by selling part of their land instead of taking out a reverse mortgage.

"This allows them to actually divide the land and capitalize on that equity right away," she said.

Reber called the ordinance one small step toward getting the county on track to meet its growth targets. "We also want to be able to show that, for simpler land division, you can do it administratively without sacrificing any of the environmental standards or other regulations that are in place," she said.
