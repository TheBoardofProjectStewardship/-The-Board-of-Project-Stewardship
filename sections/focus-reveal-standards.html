The Board of Project Stewardship Contractor evaluation standards
Read with intention

Focus on what can be verified.

Contractor evaluation becomes more useful when broad impressions give way to specific questions: who owns the work, what is documented, which records are current, and where assumptions still need independent confirmation.

Focus reveal

Move across the passage with a pointer, keyboard, or touch. Each word comes into focus individually, reinforcing a simple principle: useful contractor evaluation happens one verifiable detail at a time.

A responsible contractor evaluation should look beyond polished photographs and confident promises. Verify registration, insurance, permit responsibility, scope clarity, references, communication practices, change-order procedures, payment structure, local experience, technical coordination, and who will remain accountable when conditions change. No single badge, testimonial, estimate, or sales conversation can replace current records, written documentation, project-specific professional advice, and careful questions before construction begins.

Evaluation standards

Turn impressions into evidence.

The purpose of due diligence is not to eliminate every uncertainty. It is to understand what has been verified, what remains unknown, and who is responsible for each important part of the project.

01 · Registration

Check current public records.

Verify Washington contractor registration through Washington State Department of Labor & Industries rather than relying on old screenshots, copied license numbers, or marketing claims.

02 · Scope

Understand exactly what is included.

Compare written scope, exclusions, allowances, owner responsibilities, and assumptions. Ambiguity before signing often becomes disagreement after work starts.

03 · Stewardship

Know who owns coordination.

Identify the person responsible for selections, field questions, subcontractor handoffs, schedule changes, documentation, inspections, and unresolved issues.

04 · Permits

Define permit responsibility early.

Confirm who researches requirements, submits applications, responds to corrections, coordinates inspections, manages revisions, and closes out the permit record.

05 · Changes

Ask how surprises become decisions.

Understand the process for documenting unforeseen conditions, pricing changes, obtaining approval, updating schedule impacts, and preserving a record of what changed.

06 · Local context

Match experience to the property.

Relevant local knowledge can include jurisdiction procedures, access constraints, coastal exposure, drainage, inspection sequencing, existing conditions, and neighborhood-specific construction challenges.

Stewardship principle

The strongest question is rarely “Does this look good?” It is “What can I verify before I depend on it?”
